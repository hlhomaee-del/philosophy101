Section 2

What Is Philosophy?

Philosophy is disciplined inquiry into fundamental questions about truth, reality, knowledge, morality, mind, justice, meaning, and reasoning. It pushes us beyond first reactions and asks us to justify what we believe.

Why this matters: if you cannot explain why you believe something, you are easier to manipulate.

How philosophy differs from ordinary opinion

Ordinary OpinionPhilosophical Thinking
"I just feel this is true.""What reasons support this claim, and are they enough?"
"Everyone says so.""Popularity does not prove truth."
"That is just my view.""Can your view survive questioning?"
Section 3

Philosophical Questions

Not every question is philosophical. Some questions ask for facts. Some ask for practical methods. Philosophical questions examine concepts, standards, meanings, assumptions, and justification.

Three kinds of questions

Factual

Example: What year was the U.S. Constitution signed?

Needs evidence, records, or data.

Practical

Example: How can a city reduce traffic?

Needs strategy, planning, trade-offs.

Philosophical

Example: What makes a law just?

Needs conceptual analysis and reasoning.

Section 4

Critical Thinking Foundations

Critical thinking means examining how a claim is built, not merely whether you like it. Strong thinkers value clarity, precision, evidence, consistency, relevance, and fair-mindedness.

The six habits of a stronger thinker

Clarity
Can the claim be understood clearly?
Precision
Is the wording exact rather than vague?
Evidence
What supports the claim?
Consistency
Does the speaker contradict themselves?
Assumptions
What is being taken for granted?
Relevance
Does the reason actually support the conclusion?
Section 5

Arguments: Premises and Conclusions

An argument in philosophy is not a fight. It is a set of statements where some statements support another statement.

Premise: a supporting reason.
Conclusion: the claim the argument is trying to establish.

Quick structure clue words

Premise CluesConclusion Clues
because, since, given that, fortherefore, so, thus, hence, it follows that
Section 6

Assumptions, Ambiguity, and Flawed Reasoning

Weak arguments often depend on hidden assumptions, vague language, or logical mistakes. Strong thinkers slow down and expose what is buried underneath the wording.

Three common traps

Hidden Assumption
A claim depends on something unstated.
Ambiguity
A key word has more than one meaning.
Flawed Reasoning
The support does not really justify the conclusion.
Section 7

Building Better Arguments

You do not become a strong thinker only by spotting flaws. You become stronger by revising weak arguments into better ones.

Improve an argument by making the claim clearer, adding support, defining key terms, removing emotional shortcuts, and addressing obvious objections.

Weak version

"School uniforms are bad because students hate them."

Stronger version

"School uniform policies may be unjustified when they limit student expression without evidence that they improve safety, focus, or fairness."

Section 8

Objections and Counterarguments

A serious argument must face resistance. An objection identifies a weakness. A counterargument gives an alternative line of reasoning. Good philosophy does not avoid disagreement. It works through it.

How disagreement becomes productive

  1. State the original claim fairly.
  2. Identify the strongest objection, not the weakest.
  3. Respond directly to the objection.
  4. Revise the original claim if needed.

Section 12

Rapid Review

Use these cards when you need to reset your mind and remember how a strong thinker approaches a claim.

If you see: "Everyone says so."
Think: Popularity is not proof.
If you see: a strong emotion.
Think: Feeling is not the same as evidence.
If you see: a conclusion.
Think: What premises support it?
If you see: vague words.
Think: What exactly does that term mean?
If you see: a confident claim.
Think: What assumption is hidden here?
If you see: a weak argument.
Think: How would I revise it fairly?
Section 13

Think Like the Philosophers: Real Life Application

This section is not about memorizing names. It is about learning different frameworks for thinking better in real life.

Socrates

Core move: question assumptions.

Real life: when someone says, “If they cared, they would text all day,” ask what assumption links texting to caring.

Plato

Core move: distinguish appearance from reality.

Real life: social media may show the image of a life, not the truth of it.

Aristotle

Core move: develop good judgment and character.

Real life: courage lies between cowardice and recklessness.

Descartes

Core move: doubt what is uncertain.

Real life: do not accept a headline just because it is dramatic.

Kant

Core move: ask whether a rule should apply universally.

Real life: if everyone cheated, trust and evaluation would collapse.

Mill

Core move: consider consequences for overall well-being.

Real life: which policy reduces harm for the greatest number?
